# 1. Introduction

**Draft status.** This is a paper-shaped introduction outline for the PointStream submission. Its candidate research contributions are hypotheses pending matched, full-wire evaluation; they are not findings. The most recent project status says that a confirmed rate–distortion win has not been established, and that no tested 48-frame point is claimable in the current tennis campaign ([project README](../../README.md), [24 September campaign record](08-evidence-ledger.md)).

## Problem and scope

Broadcast tennis is a demanding but interpretable setting for testing object-centric video coding. A 4K camera follows a point through pan, tilt, and zoom. The image contains a court and venue, two articulated players, and small fast-moving objects such as the ball and racket. The camera motion can make court lines and stands move across the frame even when much of the physical scene is static. The codec must preserve what viewers use to follow play while accounting for every byte needed to reconstruct the delivered frames.

The research question is whether a semantic stream can offer a useful rate, perceptual quality, action fidelity, and client-computation tradeoff on broadcast tennis compared with reproducible generative codecs. Strong conventional codecs remain anchors: beating them is a desirable additional result, not the only possible contribution. Every comparison counts its background or venue representation, player appearance, motion, object masks, metadata, corrective residuals, and any assets or updates that must reach the client. This question does not assume that conventional codecs lack scene memory: modern codec tools can retain and reuse reference pictures. The baseline must be allowed its supported reference modes under a declared memory and latency budget ([PointStream thesis and empirical boundary](06-evaluation.md)).

The primary study is broadcast tennis. An egocentric demonstration can illustrate component integration, but it is secondary evidence and cannot establish performance on broadcast points. Claims must remain within the scenes, codecs, quality measure, and transport accounting actually evaluated.

## From selective restoration to semantic streaming

PRESLEY motivates the rate-allocation question. It preserves frame geometry while degrading selected blocks in place and sends a level map to guide client restoration. The method builds on selective degradation and restoration; it is not generic region-of-interest coding. Its relevant constraint is an accounting one: savings from degrading the coded picture must pay for restoration data, side information, and any additional channels needed to recover the chosen quality. That balance has to be measured on the restored output. See the [PRESLEY design](../../../68e8b6bb11d0dd9e62a67aef/sections/presley.tex) (repository revision `07b46952`) for the degradation map, restoration path, and transmitted side information.

GenStream explores a more radical representation. It sends performer keypoints and camera parameters and reconstructs a skater against a pre-scanned 3D venue using a generative model. Its paper reports over 99.9% reduction for the continuous stream in its figure-skating example, conditional on venue data and model weights already being distributed to clients. It describes the validation as partial and excludes the initial distribution of those assets from that continuous-stream figure (GenStream, revision `05ffd90a` (historical sibling source; see the recovery dossier)). The result motivates semantic transmission while leaving a practical accounting question open: how much of the apparent advantage remains when the required scene and appearance assets are included or their delivery assumptions are made explicit?

PointStream asks that question for broadcast tennis, where the venue and players are not assumed to arrive as a pre-scanned, event-provided 3D package. Its design can combine a background representation, per-object appearance and motion, and a corrective residual, with conventional coding available as a reference or fallback. Each component is a rate-bearing choice. The central test is the end-to-end stream against a matched modern anchor, with setup and recurring costs identified separately. This framing does not claim that PointStream is the first generative human codec or that conventional coding cannot reuse background references. Prior work already includes full-body and other generative coding systems; the candidate contribution is a tested, costed system question in the chosen tennis setting, not a priority claim ([audited manuscript positioning, `21d887c`](../../../67a9ea6275d3d9785ce57026/sections/introduction.tex); see also [related-work draft, Chapter 2](02-related-work.md)).

## Preliminary motivation and its limit

An earlier conventional-codec diagnostic provides a reason to test the decomposition. It covered eight 48-frame 4K broadcast-tennis scenes from six matches and swept AVC, HEVC, AV1, and VVC operating points. Players occupied a mean `1.11 ± 0.32%` of frame area. Removing them with plate inpainting changed the coded target and produced mean BD-rate reductions of `17.0 ± 3.1%` for AVC, `18.3 ± 3.4%` for HEVC, `15.4 ± 2.8%` for AV1, and `14.2 ± 2.6%` for VVC. The effect varied sharply by scene: the measured reductions included `1.1%` and `9.9%` cases. The diagnostic therefore indicates potential headroom in those conventional coding arms; it does not show that a semantic stream can restore the players within that headroom.

The same study compared a JPEG panorama plus per-frame homographies with conventional coding of background video, reporting `64.3–78.0%` BD-rate reductions. Those background rows have different eligible sample counts (`n=6–8`) and also describe a background-only target. They are not whole-scene PointStream results. Both measurements use BD-rate and arm-specific source scoring, so their percentages cannot be read as an available byte budget for reconstruction at matched whole-frame quality. The manuscript records the setup and caveats in its [problem section](../../../67a9ea6275d3d9785ce57026/sections/problem.tex) and [measurement appendix](../../../67a9ea6275d3d9785ce57026/appendices/headroom_measurement.tex), revision `21d887c`; it cites the external result `outputs/bp21-headroom/report.json`, which was not independently reopened for this draft.

The current project evidence supplies the necessary counterweight: the latest project plan and campaign report say that no tested 48-frame PointStream point clears the claim criterion. These results do not invalidate the conventional headroom diagnostic, but they do prevent it from being presented as a codec win ([active plan](../../PLAN.md), [campaign record](08-evidence-ledger.md)). The introduction should frame headroom as a motivation to test, then let full-wire results decide whether the opportunity survives.

## Candidate contributions as hypotheses

The following are proposed contributions to test, not established claims:

1. **H1 — full-wire rate–distortion:** On ordinary broadcast-tennis windows, a PointStream configuration can match or improve the selected quality target at a lower total transmitted rate than the matched conventional anchor when all required object, background, metadata, residual, and update bytes are charged.
2. **H2 — component value:** At least one object, background, or restoration component improves the chosen end-to-end rate–distortion measure when enabled in a controlled ablation, including its own side information and dependencies.
3. **H3 — useful amortization:** If shared scene or appearance assets have an upfront delivery cost, the measured per-point savings are positive and recover that cost within an observed and stated number of subsequent points. If recurring PointStream traffic is not cheaper than the recurring anchor traffic, there is no finite break-even point.

4. **H4 — generative-codec frontier:** Against qualified MTTF and general-video generative baselines, explicit tennis object representations improve action fidelity or perceptual quality at matched complete rate and a declared client budget. A content-type extension alone is insufficient: ablations must show why the representation helps, including failures on small players, rackets, balls, occlusions, and camera motion. A resource advantage is a separate measured hypothesis, not inferred from parameter counts.

The study should report a negative result when these hypotheses fail, and scope any positive result to the tested content and protocol. Dataset publication, a general cross-domain claim, and superiority over all generative codecs are not asserted here. The intended paper structure continues with [related work](02-related-work.md), [method](04-method.md), [implementation](05-implementation.md), [evaluation](06-evaluation.md), [experiment plan](07-experiment-plan.md), and an [evidence ledger](08-evidence-ledger.md).

## Evaluation contract

[Chapter 6](06-evaluation.md) governs prospective comparisons: complete delivered streams, separate perceptual and fidelity metrics, common-support rate–quality curves, object/task checks, and measured client resources. The old weighted-PSNR point gate remains the interpretation of historical campaign results. It is not retroactively replaced to turn a failed result into a win. Any eventual paper claim must state its comparator, metric, content, operating range, and deployment assumptions.
